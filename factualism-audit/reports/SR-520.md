## 2. Analysis — Accepted candidates
### A01 — ASIC parliamentary statement updates enforcement position
ACCEPTED — directly retrieved official statement to the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Corporations and Financial Services, 27 November 2026, enforcement section.
ASIC states it commenced proceedings concerning AIG complaint handling during 2021–25, including an alleged delay exceeding 300 days in one case. ASIC also reports civil proceedings regarding Commonwealth Bank Commbiz charges and alleges the issue persisted despite complaints and an earlier internal audit. It reports that Macquarie admitted deficiencies in promoting its Welcome Rate offer and that ASIC seeks a $14 million penalty.
Evidence classification: verified regulator representation of proceedings, allegations and reported admission; no final adjudication established here. Counter-evidence: the cases are separate, with different procedural status; the speech does not contain defendants' complete responses. The requested penalty is not an imposed penalty.
Inference: Detection, escalation, correction and restitution require separate verification. Unknown: pleadings, defences, final orders, actual remediation and historical duplication.
Source: https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/speeches/parliamentary-joint-committee-on-corporations-and-financial-services-opening-statement-27-november-2026

### A02 — Superannuation complaint intelligence fails to identify recurring issues
ACCEPTED RETROSPECTIVELY — original ASIC release 26-265MR, associated REP 844 dated 2 November 2026 in regulatory tracker.
ASIC compared 45 regulated trustees and examined 10 in greater depth. Almost a quarter did not regularly analyse complaint data for systemic issues. Over two years, 24 of 45 identified fewer than five systemic issues, including 14 identifying none. ASIC found examples of trustees too readily attributing complaint causes to members. ASIC says AFCA detected systemic issues per complaint at almost twice the rate of trustees.
Evidence: regulator review findings, not court determinations. Counter-evidence: low counts alone do not prove poor detection; different complaint populations can affect comparisons. ASIC identifies existing enforceable complaint analysis and escalation requirements under RG 271. Unknown: full report methodology, trustee-level outcomes, actual undetected harm, remediation and earlier historical admission.
Inference: closing a complaint and learning from the complaint are distinct functions; a system can fail to detect a recurring problem without recording a failure to detect it.
Sources: https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-265mr-asic-calls-out-super-trustees-for-blind-spots-in-overseeing-member-complaints

### A03 — Persistent private credit disclosure deficiencies after earlier surveillance
ACCEPTED RETROSPECTIVELY — ASIC release 26-273MR; REP 846 dated 10 November 2026 in tracker. Review November 2025–August 2026.
ASIC reviewed 24 funds managing $18 billion. Nine of 12 retail funds did not clearly disclose key decision-relevant information; 18 of 24 used multilayer intermediary structures. Six interim stop orders and enforcement referrals resulted. ASIC reports that those stop orders were lifted after identified issues were addressed; it observed some improved practices and most sampled advice demonstrated compliance.
Evidence: regulator surveillance and reported interventions. Unknown: representative industry prevalence, actual investor losses, durable compliance and final enforcement outcomes. No finding that all complex structures are improper.
Inference: disclosure availability is not necessarily intelligibility; earlier guidance is not sufficient evidence of continuing compliance.
Sources: https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-273mr-asic-finds-private-credit-practices-continue-to-leave-investors-in-the-dark

### A04 — Delegated lead generation enters regulated advice processes
ACCEPTED RETROSPECTIVELY — ASIC release 26-270MR; REP 845 dated 9 November 2026 in tracker. Review January 2025–June 2026.
ASIC examined 15 selected financial advice licensees. Lead generators referred 7,639 new clients, representing 35% of new clients in the reviewed businesses; more than $678 million was invested or switched following referrals, approximately 90% from superannuation savings. ASIC found instances of unqualified or unlicensed lead generators participating in fact finding, risk profiling and discussion of advice, and weaknesses in access to call recordings and supervision.
Evidence: regulator review, not a finding against every reviewed business. Counter-evidence: the review was targeted, lead generation itself is not necessarily unlawful, and ASIC identifies existing enforcement and proposed reforms. Unknown: individual financial losses, actual appropriateness of each switch, compliance after intervention. $678 million is transaction volume, not loss.
Inference: the licensee's responsibility must remain visible when customer-facing activities are delegated.
Sources: https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-270mr-asic-s-final-warning-to-licensees-using-lead-generators-may-put-your-licence-at-risk

### A05 — Auditor-General publishes oversight of audit quality, including changes in measurement
ACCEPTED — original ANAO Audit Quality Report 2025–26, published 26 November; evaluation signed 20 November. Sections 2.104–2.119, quality assurance strategy note a.
The report identifies two elevated or high-risk quality items concerning audit approaches and adequacy of evidence obtained from auditees. It records root-cause analysis, evidence requirements for closure of corrective actions and reciprocal performance audit reviews with the Victorian Auditor-General's Office. It discloses the cessation of an annual review-coverage indicator and a move from individual finding severity ratings to a risk-based quality assessment. The annual external ASIC reviews remained paused in 2025–26, citing resourcing challenges associated with managing a perceived independence threat.
Positive counter-evidence: the Auditor-General concluded that the quality management system operated effectively, with reasonable assurance, and reported remediation. All audit opinions and conclusions subject to monitoring were considered appropriate. The report describes external peer review of two performance audits by Audit Office New Zealand; these are not equivalent to reinstating ASIC reviews.
Evidence: published self-evaluation and reported review findings, not an independent re-audit. Unknown: subsequent review arrangements, comparability under the changed measures, individual corrective evidence.
Inference: oversight bodies should disclose their own evidence risks, assessment changes and correction criteria; assurance is bounded rather than absolute.
Source: https://www.anao.gov.au/work/corporate/audit-quality-report-2025-26

### A06 — Historical legal compliance and remediation register across shared government services
ACCEPTED RETROSPECTIVELY — original ANAO Interim Report on Key Financial Controls of Major Entities, Report 42 of 2025–26, published 25 June 2026. Summary and paragraphs 4.22.10–4.22.18.
The ANAO identified significant legislative breaches involving DSS, Health and Services Australia. In DSS's shared-service arrangements, it found some payment compliance issues known for years without adequate remediation. Services Australia's programme register contained 93 DSS-related matters on 21 November 2025, of differing risk and significance, including potential or actual constitutional breaches. The report records four matters resolved by legislative amendments, including income apportionment, and identifies other matters requiring legislation, operational or policy fixes or historical remediation.
Positive counter-evidence: ANAO records strengthened roles, reporting and legal risk processes; across 27 audited entities there were 86 interim findings versus 118 in the previous interim period and 23 prior-year findings resolved. ANAO recommends ongoing assurance over delegated service delivery and remediation. Legislative resolution does not establish actual restitution for every affected person.
Evidence: independent interim audit findings at stated dates. Unknown: final-year assessment, current register count, affected people, payments and consequential restoration. The 93 matters are not 93 proven breaches.
Inference: an institution may identify and formally register a legal compliance issue well before completing corrective action.
Source: https://www.anao.gov.au/work/financial-statement-audit/interim-report-key-financial-controls-of-major-entities-2025-26

## 3. Findings — longitudinal analysis
M01 — Complaint intelligence. A02 provides direct regulatory evidence of inadequate systemic detection and premature attribution of root causes to customers. Compare SR-512 (classification displacement), SR-515 (substantive consideration) and SR-519 (statutory systemic escalation). These are different legal contexts, not proof of a common motive.
M02 — Delegation and corrective continuity. A03 and A04 distinguish understandable disclosure, continuing compliance and licensee oversight from formal arrangements. A01 provides additional enforcement leads but does not establish final liability.
M03 — Oversight quality. A05 is constructive evidence of an accountability institution exposing its own evidence weaknesses, corrective arrangements and measurement changes. Its assurance conclusion and external review limitations must coexist.
M04 — Remediation latency. A06 documents that registering compliance problems is not equivalent to correcting them. Compare SR-518 historical NDIA debt revocations and SR-519 shared-service protection discontinuity. Individual restitution, legal correction and prevention of recurrence require separate evidence.
M05 — Chronology. Current AHRC indices show different dates for the already examined deepfake announcement; indexed ANAO care audit status differs from the directly retrieved published report. Preserve both without alleging deliberate manipulation.
No new correction of a prior MAYHEM finding established. No coordinated misconduct or new Bellbrook-specific failure established.

## 4. Conclusion
The strongest incremental finding is that ASIC identifies weaknesses in complaint analysis capable of obscuring recurring service failures, including customer attribution without adequate institutional root-cause examination. The ANAO reports establish a complementary need to examine oversight quality and distinguish documented compliance problems from completed remediation. Findings are case-isolated. No prediction or simulation.
