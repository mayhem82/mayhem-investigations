Public investigation update · 25 November 2026 · record 02

What the latest evidence adds

New records extend the disruption timeline, show local services remaining paused while the upstream service was recovering, and document an observed phishing campaign. They do not establish that the separate disruptions had one cause.

This is an addition to the earlier dated explanation, which remains available. The statements below are attributed to their publishers and do not replace the underlying evidence.

The cargo service was disrupted before it was used as a fallback

Australia's agriculture department reported that, as of approximately 9:12 am Australian Eastern Daylight Time on 4 November, an unplanned disruption affected cargo lodgements submitted for biosecurity assessment. It said importers and customs brokers might have experienced delays in receiving assessment outcomes, and that its teams used workarounds for some reference-number issues. It reported the services restored at 11:45 am on 5 November.

This was before the messaging disruption on 13 November, when the department offered the cargo lodgement enquiry function as a fallback for some documents. The department then reported separate lodgement disruptions on 15 and 23 November. This sequence shows that the fallback had its own reported availability problems; it does not establish a shared cause, a count of delayed shipments or when every affected person received an outcome.

A council kept some services paused after Microsoft began recovering

Hamilton City Council in New Zealand reported that online payments and applications would remain paused over the weekend, even if Microsoft's services were restored sooner. At 10:30 am local time on 16 November it said submissions received during the outage had been queued and were being processed. At 2 pm it reported its payment and application systems operating normally.

That gives a dated account of the council's recovery, not proof that every queued application or payment was completed. The council's statement that no information had been lost is its own report; the investigation has not independently checked each transaction.

Outage-themed phishing was observed

Security researchers at Trellix reported seeing 252 emails posing as Microsoft service notices reach more than 30 organisations between approximately 17:04 and 20:38 Coordinated Universal Time on 13 November. The researchers described a fake sign-in route. This is the activity their service reported observing, not a count of every message sent worldwide.

Microsoft also reported an outage-themed phishing campaign. The reports identify the same three sender domains and an intermediary destination. That overlap between the reports does not by itself establish that the researchers observed independent campaigns, identify an operator, prove an account was compromised, or show that the phishing caused the Microsoft outage.

Artificial-intelligence services had separate reported disruptions

Cloudflare reported that Replicate's input uploads were degraded and model builds and uploads through its registry were unavailable during an incident that began on 11 November and was marked resolved on 13 November. In a separate incident beginning on 17 November, it reported failing or stalled predictions; its updates said predictions using OpenAI models had recovered while other models could still be affected, before the broader incident was marked resolved on 18 November.

These are Cloudflare's service reports. They document different recovery stages; they do not establish that the incidents shared a cause with the Microsoft outage, that there was a compromise, or that all affected customers had recovered.

What this does not yet answer

  • How many people experienced delayed cargo, payments, applications or other consequences, and whether those individual matters were resolved.
  • Whether the separate incidents had any technical or organisational connection.
  • Whether any recipient's credentials were actually captured or an account compromised in the reported phishing activity.
  • Why the cargo-lodgement disruptions occurred and whether the later incidents shared their cause.
