What we know so far

Several major technology disruptions have affected different services. The records show real interruptions and reported workarounds. They do not establish that all these events had one cause, or that a cyberattack caused them. The investigation is still gathering evidence.

This page explains the evidence in the Technology Disruption Surge, Cybercriminal Signals and Critical Infrastructure Dependencies investigation. The links open the case records, where you can follow the source and its limits. A public statement shows what its publisher reported; some records still require verification.

Microsoft services and the people relying on them

On 12 and 13 November 2026, Microsoft 365 services experienced disruption. A university-published copy of a Microsoft service notice records a period of impact and gradual restoration. That record does not establish when every organisation or person recovered.

Health organisations described different experiences. In England, one health trust suspended an online self-referral route and later said services were returning, with slower responses possible. An Essex care board reported email disruption and gave a phone route for urgent referrals; later that day it warned that recovery was uneven and asked senders to confirm urgent referrals that had not received a response.

Other services reported boundaries on the disruption. East Midlands Ambulance Service said emergency calls were unaffected but non-emergency patient transport phone lines were busier. South East Coast Ambulance Service reported disruption to internal communication tools, while saying its call triage and ambulance dispatch remained operational. These accounts do not tell us every patient's outcome.

Evidence: Microsoft service notice reproduced by the university · health trust · Essex care board · East Midlands ambulance service · South East Coast ambulance service

Work and pay

Universities reported that the Workday service was unavailable and asked some staff to keep a separate record of hours. Brown University later reported that access had returned while automated processing was still catching up. We do not yet have evidence that every worker's hours or pay were processed correctly and on time.

Evidence: Washington University notice · Brown University notice and recovery

Australian cargo: when a fallback also became unavailable

On 13 November, Australia's agriculture department reported a messaging disruption and suggested the online cargo lodgement enquiry function as an alternative route for some treatment documents. On 15 November, it separately reported the cargo lodgement system unavailable and later said access to several systems had been restored.

The department reported another cargo lodgement disruption on 23 November and later that morning said access had been restored. Its notices do not establish that all failed submissions were received, that every shipment met its deadline, or that these incidents shared a cause. The messaging notice referred to Microsoft Dynamics 365; it does not by itself prove that it was the same service-level incident as the Microsoft 365 outage.

Evidence: messaging disruption and workaround · messaging restoration · 15 November lodgement outage · restoration · 23 November disruption · restoration

Flights and banking: separate records, separate questions

The United States Federal Aviation Administration reported that a nationwide ground stop on 13 November followed telecommunications instability during maintenance. It said the investigation into the underlying cause was continuing and that the information then available showed no indication of a cyberattack. The agency's cancellation figure includes flights it said were not necessarily cancelled because of the ground stop.

On 16 November, financial technology provider Plaid reported errors affecting some connections to Chase. A separate news report attributed an internal technical explanation to a Chase spokesperson. These records do not establish bank-wide customer outcomes or a common cause with the other disruptions.

Evidence: aviation agency account · Plaid notice · reported Chase statement

Criminal activity using the outage as a lure

Microsoft's threat intelligence team reported observing phishing messages on 13 November that used the Microsoft 365 outage as a pretext. Its report did not confirm credential capture, malware delivery or activity after a compromise. An attempted phishing campaign exploiting confusion is not, by itself, evidence that the outage was caused by an attack.

Evidence: Microsoft threat intelligence report

What the records say about cause

A report bearing Microsoft's name is publicly available through a third party. It describes interacting technical factors and separates the end of active service impact from backlog recovery. Its direct provenance and the technical explanation still need independent confirmation. In an uncorrected Tasmanian parliamentary record, a minister said that, to his knowledge at that time, Microsoft had not indicated a cyber matter. That is an attributed statement at that point in time, not a finding about every incident.

Evidence: third-party-hosted report · Tasmanian parliamentary record

What remains unanswered

  • How many people faced missed care, delayed pay, lost travel, failed lodgements or other consequences—and whether those problems were resolved.
  • Whether backlogs were cleared and workarounds were accessible to everyone who needed them.
  • Whether final, authenticated technical investigations support or change the operators' explanations.
  • Whether any particular incidents were connected, or instead had separate causes. Similar timing alone is not proof.
  • The original notice from IP Australia, the Australian intellectual-property agency, could not be collected during this pass because certificate verification failed. That access gap is recorded; it is not evidence that the notice or affected services did not exist.
