Five-Step Primary Source Verification

Every finding in this case traces to a specific evidence register entry. Here's how to check any one of them yourself, using no more than a browser.

Step 1 — Find the reference
Every claim that matters carries an evidence reference like EV-0006. Note it down.
Step 2 — Look it up in the register
Open DFAPTI, go to Evidence Register, and find that ID (or open data/evidence_register.json directly). Read its source_title, source_location, and evidence_description.
Step 3 — Open the original
source_location is the URL (or, for a locally preserved copy, preserved_file_reference points to the file in preserved/). Open it.
Step 4 — Check the claim against the source
Confirm what the evidence entry says the document says is actually what the document says. If it doesn't match, that's a finding in its own right — document it.
Step 5 — Record your check
Note the date you checked, the ID, and whether it held. If you're relying on this case for your own submission, this independent check strengthens it.
Worked example. Enforcement Notice Part One states Willawarrin's plant was targeted for "early 2026" completion, citing EV-0006. Step 2: EV-0006's source is an official NSW Government release, "Sod turns on new $8 million Willawarrin water treatment plant" (water.dcceew.nsw.gov.au). Step 3: open that URL. Step 4: confirm the release states an early-2026 completion target and that reservoir refurbishment follows commissioning. Step 5: record what you found and the date.

If a Document Has Moved or Disappeared

  • Search the NSW Government or Council site directly for the document title.
  • Try the Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) for the original URL.
  • For a Council record, a Government Information (Public Access) Act 2009 (NSW) request can be made directly to Kempsey Shire Council; councils must respond within 20 working days.
  • An inability to access a document is itself worth recording — note the date, the URL, and what you found or didn't.

If There's No Response, or an Inadequate One

None of this is regulatory enforcement — it's real, legitimate pressure that has moved things before. Use it in order; skipping straight to the top doesn't usually work better.

1. Office of Local Government NSW — formal complaint
If Council hasn't responded within 28 days, or the response doesn't actually answer the questions, submit a formal complaint via OLG's Council Complaint Form (olg.nsw.gov.au/public/complaints-about-councils), referencing this Enforcement Notice and the non-response. OLG has real statutory investigation powers over council governance and service delivery.
2. Your state MP — Michael Kemp, Member for Oxley
Suite 1, 72 Elbow Street, West Kempsey NSW 2440 · oxley@parliament.nsw.gov.au · (02) 6562 6190. Not a regulator, but a constituent-service channel that can raise the matter directly with the Minister for Water or Minister for Local Government, or ask a question in Parliament. Send the Enforcement Notice and a short note that this is a constituency matter for Willawarrin and Bellbrook residents.
3. Your federal MP — Pat Conaghan, Member for Cowper
Port Macquarie office: JBC Building, Suite 1, Level 4, 41-47 Horton Street, Port Macquarie NSW 2444 (PO Box 345, Port Macquarie NSW 2444) · pat.conaghan.mp@aph.gov.au · (02) 6583 8387. Relevant specifically to the National Water Grid Authority request, since that's a Commonwealth body.
4. NSW Ombudsman — last resort for the council complaint
The Ombudsman expects other avenues (Council, then OLG) to be exhausted first. If OLG's response is itself inadequate, the Ombudsman can investigate and report publicly or to Parliament. Not a first-round recipient — see ombo.nsw.gov.au/complaints.
Log every one of these the same way as the original recipients (see Logging Responses). A constituency letter that produces a ministerial enquiry, or an OLG complaint that produces a compliance direction, is a real outcome — worth recording as carefully as a direct reply would be.
