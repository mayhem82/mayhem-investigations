Willawarrin and BellbrookInvestigation active

Recurring drinking-water failures

Residents have repeatedly faced unreliable or unsafe drinking-water conditions. The investigation is examining what public bodies knew, what they did, and why the problem continued.

What we know

  • Water failures have affected both communities more than once.
  • Government and council records show years of projects, funding decisions and public responses.
  • A new treatment plant and related works have been announced and commenced.

What remains unclear

  • Whether all past risks and failures were properly recorded and communicated.
  • Whether earlier action could reasonably have prevented later harm.
  • Whether the current works will resolve the full problem for both communities.

Why this matters

Safe drinking water is essential. When service failures repeat, the public needs a clear account of the cause, the response and who remains responsible.

Kempsey and the Mid North CoastEvidence being checked

Proposed police-drone expansion

A proposal seeks to extend a police-drone model used at Moree into Kempsey and the wider Mid North Coast. The investigation is checking whether the public claims supporting that expansion are actually supported by reliable evidence.

What we know

  • A police-drone trial has operated in Moree.
  • A petition has called for the model to be extended to Kempsey and the Mid North Coast.
  • The material collected so far includes claims that still require independent confirmation.

What remains unclear

  • What measurable results the Moree trial has produced.
  • Whether those results can fairly be applied to Kempsey and surrounding communities.
  • What safeguards, oversight and public reporting would accompany an expansion.

Why this matters

New police technology can affect safety, privacy, spending and public trust. Expansion should be based on demonstrated need and verified results, not repetition alone.

BellbrookEvidence being checked

Years without a flying-fox management plan

A protected flying-fox colony has affected Bellbrook on and off since 2019. The species can't be forcibly moved on - but that's not the whole story. A real government process exists to manage the impact on residents and unlock state funding for it. Kempsey Shire Council hasn't used it.

What we know

  • A colony moved into Bellbrook in November 2019. Council's own records confirm it left again in October 2021 - so this isn't one unbroken six-year presence, and this site no longer states it that way.
  • In May 2025, a community request for council to develop a formal management plan met a mixed response - accounts differ on exactly what was agreed, but every account agrees council did not commit to the plan itself.
  • The NSW Government holds about $1 million to help councils manage exactly this kind of impact - but a council can only access it for a site that has a completed or draft management plan. A nearby town's plan, completed in 2022, means it could apply; Bellbrook's cannot, because it has none.
  • In June 2026, a formal request was sent to the Commonwealth department asking that this colony be added to the national flying-fox monitoring program - the first attempt of its kind for this colony, and something no resident had previously been told was an option. The department's reply: that national program hasn't had funding, and hasn't been actively monitoring camps, since 2022. It suggested trying the state government instead.

What remains unclear

  • Whether the colony that prompted the 2025-2026 concern is a return after the 2021 departure, or something else - and whether council's current website, which reportedly still lists Bellbrook as a "historic" (no longer active) camp, is accurate or out of date.
  • What was actually agreed at the May 2025 council meeting - accounts of the same meeting differ, and this site is treating the most specific one as unconfirmed until it can be checked against the actual signed minutes.
  • Whether council intends to develop a management plan for Bellbrook at all, or on what timeline.
  • The extent of any health impact from the colony's droppings on nearby properties - the underlying risk is medically real, but no local case has been confirmed either way.

Why this matters

A community has lived with a real, recurring impact for years while the one process that could unlock formal help for it has sat unused. That's a gap in what got done, not a dispute about what the law allows.

BellbrookFormal notice drafted

A laneway nobody will take responsibility for

Council has been maintaining a laneway that some Bellbrook residents rely on as their only access - while telling those same residents it isn't council's problem, because the land is Crown land. A real process exists to formally resolve exactly this kind of situation. In over 130 years, nobody has used it.

What we know

  • Council's own published records - work updates, its bin collection schedule, its capital works program - show it maintaining the laneway and the street next to it.
  • When a resident raises a maintenance concern, council points to Crown land status as the reason it isn't responsible. The state Crown Lands agency's own public guidance, in turn, points enquiries back to the local council.
  • A real, existing state government process would let the laneway be formally transferred to council, resolving exactly this kind of gap - and council's own maintenance work already meets the test for using it. Neither council nor the state agency has ever done so.

What remains unclear

  • Whether the laneway is actually Crown land - that classification, which council relies on to explain its position, has never been formally confirmed by any government record located so far.
  • Whether a formal notice about this has already been sent to council, or is still waiting to be sent - the investigation's own material gives conflicting signals on this point.

Why this matters

For the residents who depend on it, this laneway is their only way in or out. When two authorities each point to the other and neither takes the one formal step that would settle the matter, the person left without an answer is the resident - not either authority.

Taylors ArmFormal notice drafted

A complaint, made through the correct channel. A tracking register that never once recorded it.

In March 2023, a Taylors Arm resident raised a flying-fox colony with council at a public meeting - the formal channel council itself provides for exactly this purpose - and asked that the trees it was roosting in, which council is separately required to control as a weed, be removed. Council's own minutes record the request. Four separate checks of council's own tracking register across the following three years, spanning a change of mayor, show no entry for it at any point.

What we know

  • A named resident formally raised the issue at a council meeting on 16 March 2023, with specific documented harms - contaminated tank drinking water, damaged property, no usable outdoor space. Council's own minutes record it and nothing after it.
  • Four separate tracking registers, spanning over two years and a change of mayor, show no entry for this matter at any point. The current council has no formal record the complaint was ever made.
  • The neighbouring council next door used a formal environmental assessment process to remove the same species of tree, for the same reason, in 2024. No equivalent process appears in any Nambucca Valley Council document.
  • The same pattern - an active colony, no management plan, no confirmed funding access - is documented at the same time 27 kilometres away at Bellbrook, in a different council area. See that case above.
  • A Commonwealth process exists that can, in principle, support funding access for situations like this - but nothing in the public record shows any Taylors Arm resident being told about it. When the equivalent request was made for the Bellbrook colony in June 2026, the department's reply was that the program has had no funding since 2022.

What remains unclear

  • Whether the colony has grown, shrunk, or moved since 2023 - there is no public record of its current size or exact location.
  • Whether council responded to the resident privately, outside the public record - a formal records request would be needed to find out.

Why this matters

The register that exists specifically to carry unresolved matters forward - between meetings, and between one council term and the next - contains no trace of this one. That's what breaks institutional memory: a resident raising this again today would be starting from a council that has no record it was ever raised before.

Kempsey Shire CouncilReference resource

Why council records are hard to check

Checking any of the investigations on this site means reading Council's own material - and that material is written in a private vocabulary of acronyms, codes and reference numbers that often aren't explained anywhere public. This entry explains what that looks like and links to the plain-language key being built to work through it.

What we know

  • Reading Council's own published material means encountering hundreds of acronyms, codes and reference numbers - policy numbers, file numbers, tender codes, work-order numbers, resolution identifiers - that are rarely explained where they appear.
  • Some of these codes mean two different things depending on which Council document they appear in, with nothing in the document itself to say which meaning applies.
  • No public, plain-language key connecting these terms across Council's own material was found published by Council itself.
  • A working glossary translating this vocabulary is being built and checked against Council's own primary documents, term by term - over 400 entries so far, with more research passes ongoing.

What remains unclear

  • Whether this is an ordinary side-effect of how a large organisation's records build up over decades, an oversight, or a deliberate choice - the available information doesn't say, and this site won't guess.
  • Whether Council holds an internal key or glossary that simply isn't published for residents.
  • How many people, in practice, give up trying to check a specific council claim, cost or decision because the terminology needed to do so isn't accessible without doing this kind of groundwork first.

Why this matters

Whatever the reason behind it, the practical effect is the same either way: someone trying to verify a specific council decision, cost or process against the public record first has to learn a private vocabulary before they can even find the right document, let alone read it. That is a real barrier to the kind of ordinary accountability this site exists to support, regardless of why the barrier exists.

What you get, even without a result

You don't have to wait for a complaint to be answered to already have something.

A formal complaint or notice can sit unanswered for months. That's real, and it's documented on this site where it happens. But every investigation above already gives you something the moment it's published - whether or not anyone in government ever replies.

  1. 01

    A plain account of what happened

    Not a legal filing, not a press release from either side - what the record actually shows, in ordinary language.

  2. 02

    Evidence you can check yourself

    Every claim traces back to a real source. You don't have to take this site's word for it, and you're shown exactly how to check.

  3. 03

    A name for the pattern, once you've seen it you can't unsee it

    When the same kind of failure shows up in more than one place, this site names the pattern rather than treating each case as a one-off.

  4. 04

    Something to hand to the next person

    Every page here is written so someone with no background in any of this can pick it up, understand it, and act on it - or pass it to someone who can.

This is why every case on this site keeps working - keeps being worth reading - even on the ones where nobody in government has responded yet. Full technical detail: the Yield Catalogue.

Integrity audits

Checking our own work

This site holds itself to the same standard it applies to Council and NSW Police: claims traced to evidence, distinguished from judgement, with gaps disclosed rather than concealed. This is the running log of self-checks against that standard, newest first, applying the same Decision Register discipline explained in full on the Governance page. More gets added as this work continues.

25 August 2026Site-wide

Does the governance rules page actually match the repository's own architecture?

Checked the newly-published Governance page against the repository it describes. One principle - "Single Case: only one investigation is active at a time" - directly contradicted the repository's actual state: five concurrently active, isolated cases, and two of them now running their own separate scheduled automation.

Result: the rule was wrong, not the repository. Corrected the specification itself, not just the page describing it - renamed the principle "Case Isolation": a repository may run one investigation or several at once, each fully isolated, never sharing state. A separate, older internal document had drifted even further, claiming the repository "does not support multiple concurrent cases today" - corrected that too.

Does Enforcement Notice ever get described as a complaint?

Swept the site for language that frames MAYHEM's own remedy-demand document as a complaint - something already corrected once on one case, but never checked for elsewhere it could have recurred.

Result: found it had recurred. How to read this site

No specialist knowledge required.

The underlying investigation may be detailed. The public explanation should not be. Every summary answers the same ordinary questions.

  1. 01

    What happened?

    A direct description of the public issue, without institutional language.

  2. 02

    What do we actually know?

    Information supported by records that have been checked.

  3. 03

    What remains unclear?

    Missing evidence, conflicting accounts and questions that cannot yet be answered.

  4. 04

    Why does it matter?

    The practical effect on people, services, rights, safety, public money or trust.

  5. 05

    What happens next?

    The next public decision, unresolved responsibility or action needed for a proper answer.

Our public standard

Clear does not mean simplified beyond truth.

KNOWN

Supported by information that has been checked.

LIKELY

A reasonable reading, clearly separated from established information.

UNCLEAR

The available information does not give a reliable answer.

DISPUTED

Reliable accounts or records do not agree.
